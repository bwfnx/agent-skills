# NIH Clinical Trial Determination

Applies to NIH/HHS proposals involving human participants. Applicants get this wrong in a predictable direction — they talk themselves into "not a trial" because a trial designation means registration, monitoring, and extra attachments. Give the honest answer, not the convenient one.

Everything below is method. **Fetch the current NIH pages before relying on any of it** — the wording has been revised before.

## Sources to pull

- Definition and the four questions: `grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/clinical-trials/definition`
- Worked case studies: `grants.nih.gov/policy/clinical-trials/case-studies.htm`
- Decision tool (self-assessment aid, not binding): `grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/clinical-trials/ct-decision`
- Designation meanings: `grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/clinical-trials/specific-funding-opportunities`
- Registration and reporting requirements, and data and safety monitoring policy: search `grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/`

## The four questions

All four must be yes:

1. Does the study involve human participants?
2. Are the participants prospectively assigned to an intervention?
3. Is the study designed to evaluate the effect of the intervention on the participants?
4. Is the effect being evaluated a health-related biomedical or behavioral outcome?

## What does not get you out of it

NIH states explicitly that a study still meets the definition when it has a single arm and no control, uses a behavioral intervention, is no more than minimal risk, or has only one aim that qualifies. Applicants cite all four of these as reasons they aren't running a trial. None work.

Question 2 is almost always yes for anything delivering a defined intervention to enrolled participants. **Question 3 decides every real case.**

## Use the case studies, not first principles

NIH's published case studies resolve close calls far better than reasoning from the definition, because NIH's application of "designed to evaluate the effect" is narrower and broader than intuition in specific ways.

Two families matter most:

**Usability and acceptability testing of a tool** is generally *not* a trial. NIH's reasoning is that the study measures properties of the artifact rather than effects on the participant. The paradigm case is short-duration software testing.

**Feasibility and tolerability of a program** generally *is* a trial. NIH has treated determining the tolerable intensity and adequate adherence of a behavioral program as evaluating a health-related outcome.

That distinction is the crux, and pilot and feasibility studies fall into the gap. A study measuring **attendance, retention, and participant burden** is measuring adherence and tolerability — which NIH has called health-related — even when the applicant labels them "feasibility outcomes" and states plainly that no health outcome is assessed. Meanwhile **recruitment yield, data completeness, and fidelity** are properties of the research process rather than the participant, and are the cleanest non-trial outcomes available.

Note also the exclusion for studies **intended solely to refine measures**. It is real and protects instrument-validation work — but it protects a component, not a study that also delivers a full-dose intervention.

When an applicant leans on a usability case study to justify a months-long, full-dose intervention delivered to a clinical population, say directly that the analogy doesn't hold, and name the closer case.

## Consequences depend on the announcement's designation

This determines how hard to push. Verify which applies:

- **Clinical Trial Not Allowed** — a proposal proposing a trial is returned without review. Fatal; the cycle is lost.
- **Clinical Trial Required** — the mirror image.
- **Clinical Trial Optional** — accepts either. A wrong call becomes a correctable form error rather than a fatal one.

NIH has consolidated some previously paired announcements into Optional-only, which materially lowers the stakes — but this varies, and institute-specific announcements often retain the split. Check the specific announcement, and check whether the target institute accepts trials under it, since announcements sometimes accept trials generally while individual institutes do not.

Even under Optional, obligations attach: ClinicalTrials.gov registration within the required window after first enrollment, results reporting, a data and safety monitoring plan, and possibly a regulatory attachment that is *prohibited* for non-trial applications.

## The recommendation

Under an Optional announcement, **designate up**. Misclassifying as a trial costs paperwork. Misclassifying as not-a-trial risks a post-award compliance finding on registration and, more immediately, a reviewer who concludes the applicant doesn't understand their own study.

A stated intention to collect instrument scores but never analyze them is not verifiable by a reviewer and is commonly discounted — the Human Subjects and Clinical Trials form forces a declaration of outcome measures, and listing validated instruments there reads as a trial regardless of narrative disclaimers.

## On waiting for NIH to decide

Applicants stall submissions waiting for a program officer to rule. Tell them not to. **The applicant makes the determination on the form; NIH staff verify it.** One more contact attempt, then proceed on their own call.

This matters more for mechanisms that have lost late-submission eligibility — verify whether a late window exists before advising how long they can wait.

Also: if an applicant has documented a chain of unanswered emails to named program officers inside a submitted attachment, cut it. It records that they couldn't get an answer and helps nobody.
