# Review Craft

How to critique once you know the rules. The failure patterns below recur across agencies and across first-time and experienced applicants alike.

## Method

Pull the literal questions from the solicitation's evaluation criteria and answer each on the applicant's behalf, as a reviewer would. The recurring pattern is simple and devastating: **a criterion asks a direct question, the proposal never answers it, and the applicant never noticed the question was asked.** Phase-specific questions embedded inside general criteria are the usual casualties — they read like boilerplate until you look closely.

Surface the unanswered question, quote it, and say where the answer belongs.

## The universal criteria, whatever they're called

Agencies name these differently but nearly all evaluate the same four things. Map the agency's actual criteria onto these, then work each.

### Technical merit and innovation

- **Is the proposed work actually R&D?** Several agencies explicitly refuse to fund straightforward engineering, incremental product development, or work whose outcome isn't in doubt. If the proposal reads as building a known thing, that's a scope problem, not a writing problem.
- **Is there technical risk, and is it named?** Proposals that promise certain success read as either naive or as disguised product development. Reviewers want the hard part identified and a plan for it.
- **Is the method visible?** Work hidden behind "proprietary" cannot be scored. Proprietary status is not a reason agencies accept for withholding methods — data rights protect intellectual property, omission does not. Recommend disclosing architecture (theoretical grounding, instrument development, decision logic, algorithmic approach) while withholding parameters.
- **Is there a theoretical or empirical foundation?** Invented frameworks with no mapping to established theory or prior work read as unmoored. In behavioral and life-science proposals this single issue sinks more scores than any other.
- **Is preliminary data present and used?** Applicants who have run something informally routinely assert that the experience "informed refinement" without showing a number. Completion rates, performance data, failure modes, verbatim user feedback — even clearly labeled as informal and not research — are far more persuasive than the assertion. This is free and almost always available.

### Team and qualifications

Where proposals quietly die, and where honest feedback is scarcest.

- **Unnamed key personnel.** "A qualified statistical consultant," "a software engineer to be hired" — these are direct answers of *no* to a criterion asking whether the team has that expertise. Named person with a biosketch, or it doesn't count. Naming someone is usually the single highest-value change available and takes a phone call.
- **People central to the plan with no biosketch and no letter.** A clinical collaborator carrying the entire recruitment strategy, or a manufacturing partner carrying the scale-up, appearing only as a name in a resources paragraph.
- **Credential mismatch.** Accomplishments from an unrelated sector presented under a research heading invite the wrong reaction. The experience may be genuinely relevant — the framing is what fails. Recommend reframing, not removal.
- **No track record anywhere on the team.** No publications, no prior awards, no experience leading a project of this type. Survivable, but only by addition. Recommend a specific named institution and department, ideally nearby, and offer to help make the approach.
- **Commercialization capability.** Most agencies fold "can this team commercialize it" into the qualifications criterion. Technical teams with no business capability and no stated plan to acquire it lose points here.
- **Availability.** Check whether key personnel have obvious competing full-time commitments visible in their own biosketches. Reviewers notice.

### Commercial potential

- **Does it name the buyer?** Who purchases this, through what channel, at what price. "Significant unmet need" is not an answer.
- **Does it name competitors?** The criterion usually asks about advantage over existing approaches *and those in development*. Proposals routinely describe their own novelty and never name a competitor. In a crowded market this reads as ignorance of the field or evasion, and reviewers frequently work at or consult for the competing organizations.
- **Is the differentiator explicit and comparative?** It's usually present but stated abstractly. Make it concrete and make it against named alternatives.
- **Is there a revenue or reimbursement path?** For regulated or healthcare products this is where proposals are thinnest. "None yet" is an acceptable answer if the proposal says what replaces it.
- **Is the market size cited?** A sourced figure beats an adjective.

### Approach, feasibility, and environment

- **Timeline arithmetic.** Work it forward, not backward. Add up startup activities against the startup window. Check whether the last-enrolled participant or last-built unit can finish within the performance period. Check whether contingency provisions push past a stated maximum. Applicants construct schedules by working backward from the end date and never test them forward.
- **Recruitment or acquisition without a denominator.** "We will enroll N participants" or "we will secure N pilot customers" with no statement of the available pool or assumed conversion rate. Reviewers substitute their own pessimism. Recommend putting the volume figure in a partner's letter so it carries their signature rather than the applicant's assertion.
- **Statistical honesty.** For small studies, compute the confidence interval around the key estimate and state it. Justify sample size on precision and operational grounds rather than convention. Reviewers respect stated limits.
- **Milestones and go/no-go criteria.** Prespecified, quantified benchmarks tied to a named decision point are a genuine strength — say so when present. Then check for a decision rule: which are must-pass, which are informative, and what happens on partial failure. "Each evaluated independently" leaves the question open.
- **Aims that cannot fail.** An objective entirely within the applicant's control — producing a document, holding meetings, delivering a plan — reads as a deliverable, not research. It may be allowable; it still needs framing as risk reduction with defined technical objectives.
- **Business environment.** Where the criterion asks about business capability, proposals answer with office space and computers. Customer discovery, accelerator participation, and market research belong here with specifics: how many interviews, what was learned, what changed.
- **Risk without mitigation.** Every named risk needs an attached procedure, an escalation path, and a decision rule. Proposals often name a risk correctly and then never say what will be done about it.

## Writing-level problems that cost real points

- **Defensive repetition.** Count how many times a disclaiming or hedging phrase appears across the package. Repetition of a defensive claim signals anxiety and consumes page budget the technical sections need. Recommend a specific number of instances and where they go.
- **Placeholders.** `[TO BE INSERTED]`, `[TBD]`, "dates pending," blank account numbers. Common in near-final drafts and read as carelessness to a reviewer looking for reasons to lower a score.
- **Boilerplate that could describe any company.** If a paragraph would survive a find-and-replace of the company name, it isn't earning its space.
- **Thin bibliography.** Check for the landmark work and professional-society standards a specialist reviewer will expect in that field. Absence suggests the applicant doesn't know the literature. Also check for a cited methodological framework appropriate to the study type — it makes a plan read as principled rather than assembled.
- **Graphics as decoration.** Figures need action captions that make a point, not labels. A figure referenced in the text but never actually included is a missed opportunity worth flagging.
- **Wrong register for the instrument.** A grant-style research narrative submitted against a contract solicitation, or vice versa. Contract reviewers want deliverables and schedule; grant reviewers want rationale and rigor.

## Cross-document consistency

Cheap to check, expensive to leave in. Verify the same numbers appear everywhere:

- Personnel in the budget match the biosketches match the narrative match the org chart
- The timeline in the summary matches the one in the technical volume matches the one in the management plan
- Work-percentage splits in the budget actually satisfy the stated requirement
- The schedule matches the staffing plan matches the deliverables list

Inconsistencies suggest sections were written separately and never reconciled — usually true, and it shouldn't be visible.

## Compliance-critical human subjects and regulatory checks

Where applicable:

- **Vague exclusion criteria.** "Individuals whose needs exceed the scope of the study" is unevaluable. Push for the enumerated list.
- **Missing contemporary context.** Standard of care and technology baselines shift. A proposal that ignores a development now widespread in its field reads as out of date on page one. Check what has changed recently and whether the plan accounts for it — as a criterion, a covariate, or an explicit rationale for ignoring it.
- **Unstated participant compensation.** Reviewers must assess undue influence and cannot do it from "per the approved schedule."
- **Monitoring plans not labeled as the required plan.** A risk-proportionate plan is often appropriate, but it has to be identifiable as satisfying the requirement.
- **Regulatory pathway sections that describe institutional review instead of a regulatory pathway.** These are different things, and solicitations that require the latter score it directly.
